# Discovery concepts (draft, generated from concepts/discovery.json)

The named concepts under the Discovery items in `builder-inventory.md`. Finding out what is true about a problem and the people who have it, and choosing what is worth building, before spending weeks on it.

Each concept has up to four checks, each counted once:

- **Know it**: says what it is and applies it on paper. Evidence: final exam questions on the concept,
  all three types (know it, use it, predict it), and `/learn` checks. Self-rated in the survey until
  the exam. (The exam's "use it" questions are scenarios on paper; the profile's "Used it" is real work.)
- **Used it**: used it in their own work. Evidence: the `/sprint-review` session scan, or the repo.
- **Built it**: built their own. Evidence: a file they wrote, committed in a shared repo.
- **Shipped it**: made it for real users and chose it on purpose (the inventory's level 5 rules).

A blank cell means the check does not apply to that concept. Evidence rules from
`builder-inventory.md` apply.

## D3 Assumption tests

| Concept | Used it | Built it | Shipped it | Chapter |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **DI10 Belief versus truth and triangulation.** A map of what you believe, what is true, and how much evidence you have; False Confidence (sure, and wrong) is the most dangerous region. Every way of knowing can mislead, so treat a belief as validated only when independent sources agree. Good evidence would look different if you were wrong. | n/a | Built: an insights or assumptions file that lists beliefs with a confidence level and the evidence for each, written before the checks that tested them. | n/a | Ch 4: Discovery Is Truth-Seeking (Six ways of knowing, all flawed; Thinking in probabilities) |
| **DI11 Issue trees and MECE.** Break a messy question into parts that do not overlap and leave nothing out (mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive), splitting until each leaf can be checked with evidence. Work first on the branch with high impact, high uncertainty and testability, and skip any question that fails the so-what test (would the answer change what we build?). | Used: worked a question with an issue tree someone else drew (a teammate, an agent) or asked an agent to check a tree for overlaps and gaps (session scan). | Built: a committed issue tree for an open product question, with the leaf chosen to test and the questions excluded. | n/a | Ch 4: Structure a Messy Problem: Issue Trees |
| **DI12 What would have to be true.** State the idea as a hypothesis on day one, then list the specific conditions that must hold for it to work (Roger Martin). Each condition can be tested; a vague belief cannot. | n/a | Built: a committed list of conditions that would have to be true, each labeled with its risk and uncertainty. | n/a | Ch 4: The Four Risks and the Riskiest Assumption (What would have to be true) |
| **DI13 The four risks.** Value (will they want it), usability (can they use it), feasibility (can we build it), viability (does it work as a business). A MECE list of how a product fails. Teams over-test feasibility and under-test value; a working app proves only that it could be built. | n/a | Built: an assumptions list with each assumption labeled by risk, committed. | n/a | Ch 4: The Four Risks and the Riskiest Assumption (The four risks) |
| **DI14 Riskiest assumption.** The condition that is both most uncertain and most fatal if false. Test it before anything else, even before a cheaper quick win, because work on other parts is wasted if it fails. | Used: named a riskiest assumption in the sprint plan ("My riskiest assumption is ___, and this sprint I will test it by ___"). | Built: named the assumption before checking it, ran a check that could prove it wrong, and committed the result (D3 level 4). | The result decided something, and a decision record cites it (D3 level 5). | Ch 4: The Four Risks and the Riskiest Assumption (The riskiest assumption) |
| **DI15 The smallest test: smoke test, fake door, concierge, Wizard of Oz, presell.** Before building, fake it, do it by hand, or sell it first. Choose by learning per hour: the highest evidence rung on the riskiest assumption for the fewest hours. A working app shown to friends stays on the said rung for value. | Used: took part in or reused someone else's test (a teammate's landing page, a class template) and recorded its result. | Built: designed and ran one of the tests (a page, a hand-delivered service, a pre-order), with the action counted, its rung, a prediction set before, and the result committed. | Run with people outside the team, and the result decided something in a decision record. | Ch 4: The Smallest Test |

## D4 Competitive landscape

| Concept | Used it | Built it | Shipped it | Chapter |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **DI16 The current habit and the forces of progress.** The main competitor is what the person does today: a spreadsheet, a group text, or nothing. A person switches only when push plus pull outweighs anxiety plus habit (Moesta), so the product must beat the habit by more than the cost of switching. No rival app does not mean no competition. | Used: asked "What do you do today?" in interviews, recorded per person (the repo). | Built: a written comparison of what users actually do today and the alternatives they use, from interviews, including doing nothing. A competitor price list with no user input does not count. | The product's position comes from that comparison, in a decision record (D4 level 5). | Ch 4: The Real Competitor Is the Current Habit |
| **DI17 Importance versus satisfaction.** Plot needs by how much they matter and how well current solutions meet them (Olsen). The opportunity is high importance with low satisfaction; high importance with high satisfaction means competing with what already works. | n/a | Built: a committed importance and satisfaction table or chart built from interview data. | n/a | Ch 4: Evaluating Your Problems (Another lens: importance and satisfaction) |
| **DI18 Five forces.** Rivalry, new entrants and substitutes decide whether an industry can hold price above cost; buyer and supplier power decide whether a neighbor can bargain your share away (Porter). The forces describe the industry, not whether your product is strongest in it. | n/a | Built: a written five forces reading of the product's industry, committed. | n/a | Ch 3: The five forces: how wide is the band? |
| **DI19 Blue ocean and value innovation.** Create uncontested space by raising willingness to pay and cutting cost at once, dropping what the industry over-serves and adding what it never offered (Kim and Mauborgne). A blue ocean has no barriers, so it needs a moat to stay yours. | n/a | Built: a written list of factors the product drops, reduces, raises and adds compared with the alternatives, committed. | n/a | Ch 3: Blue oceans: widening the band |
| **DI20 Moats (7 Powers).** Sources of durable advantage (Helmer): network effects, switching costs, scale economies, brand, counter-positioning, cornered resource, process power. Ask what stops the next builder with the same AI tools from copying you next week. An incumbent's switching costs can close an apparent opening. | n/a | Built: a written moat statement for the product (the one-page strategy) that names a power and why it applies. | n/a | Ch 3: Moats: why the win lasts |
| **DI21 Disruption.** Incumbents improve for their most demanding customers and overshoot the rest (sustaining innovation). An entrant starts in a low-end foothold (overserved customers) or a new-market foothold (nonconsumers) and moves upmarket (Christensen). Fast growth that hurts incumbents is not disruption by itself. | n/a | Built: a written answer to the chapter's four questions: the incumbent, whom it overserves or leaves out, sustaining or disruptive entry, and what would have to improve. | n/a | Ch 3: Disruption: how small entrants beat leaders |

## Book gaps

- Decision records: D1, D3, D4 and D6 level 5 all require a decisions/ file that cites interviews or a test, but no chapter shows how to write one (options, deciding reason, evidence cited, committed before the work). Add a short section to Ch 4 after The Commitment Decision, or in Ch 6b with specs.
- Writing up a comparison of alternatives (D4 level 4): Ch 4 teaches the current habit and Ch 3 teaches five forces and moats, but nothing shows how to build a comparison table of what users do today from interview notes, as distinct from a desk-research competitor list. Add a worked table to Ch 4: The Real Competitor Is the Current Habit.
- Choosing one idea over another (D6 level 4): Diverge Before You Converge and Evaluating Your Problems score problems, but no section shows a written idea-versus-idea choice that names who it is for and why it can win. Add to Ch 4: The Commitment Decision, with an example record.
- Stopping an idea: Signs You Should Pivot lists symptoms, but there is no guidance on recording a decision to stop or shelve an idea from evidence (the pilot found such records fit no item). Add to Ch 4: Avoiding Premature Commitment.
- Ranking with criteria shown (D5 level 4): Feature Prioritization lists ICE, MoSCoW and the $100 test without a worked ranked list, and carries a MOVE note saying it belongs with specs. Add a worked example and settle where the section lives.
- Opportunity solution tree is taught in one paragraph and a figure in Discovery Is Continuous, but has a midterm question. Expand it, or retire the question.
- Surveys: D3 level 4 lists a survey as a valid check, but the book does not teach survey design or when a survey misleads (only that a concierge test teaches more than a survey). Add a short note to Ch 4: The Smallest Test.
- The brief (Ch 4: From Discovery to the Brief) is left to G4 Specs on the Design axis; if Design does not claim it, it needs a Discovery concept.

## Evidence a packet or the session scan cannot see yet

- Interview order: D1 level 4 excludes interviews that only collect the product's content, and "problem interviews before the student saw the product" needs the interview text and date. The packet often lists discovery/ files with no text (the text budget runs out). Put discovery/ files ahead of code in the packet's text budget.
- Assumption named before the check (D3 level 4) needs commit order between the assumptions file and the test note. The packet shows commit hashes but a coder cannot always tell which came first; listing each discovery file's first-commit date in the packet would settle it.
- Used it for most Discovery concepts happens outside the repo and outside Claude Code (talking to people, a whiteboard). The session scan sees only an agent asked to draft or check a guide, a persona, a tree or a ranking. A "Discovery this sprint" table in the /sprint-review template (what was done, with whom, date, file) would give the scan something to read.
- Agent-written versus student-written: a persona, synthesis or assumptions list drafted by an agent from nothing looks the same in the repo as one built from interviews. The session scan could flag discovery files an agent wrote before any interview note existed.
- Real users for Discovery level 5 often means interviewees, who have not used the product. The packet cannot tell an interviewee from a user; the "Users this sprint" table proposed in builder-inventory.md would separate them.
- Smoke tests and presells record their result as counts (visits, sign-ups, deposits) that live in a landing-page tool or Stripe, not the repo. Ask students to commit the counts with a date in the test note.
- Several Ch 3 concepts (five forces, blue ocean, moats, disruption, value stick, DHM, reflection test) are rarely written down in a student repo; their Built it rests on the one-page product strategy. Asking for that page as a committed strategy.md in Sprint 2 would make them visible.
