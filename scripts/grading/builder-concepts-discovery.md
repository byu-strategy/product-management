# Discovery concepts (draft, generated from concepts/discovery.json)

The named concepts under the Discovery items. Finding out what is true about a problem and the people who have it, and choosing what is worth building, before spending weeks on it.

Each concept is measured in up to two ways:

- **Know it** (every concept): the exams. Midterm and final questions on the concept come in three
  types (know it, use it, predict it). The September survey self-rating stands in until the midterm.
- **Practice** (every concept not marked knowledge only): the student's own work shows it, counted
  once. The **Practice** column says what counts as evidence: the `/sprint-review` session scan's
  "Builder tools you used" list, or a file in a shared repo. This is the breadth half of the profile's
  practice score; depth (`depth-levels.md`) is assigned per axis, not per concept.

A concept marked **knowledge only** has no practice check: it is measured by the exams alone.
Evidence rules are in `concept-coder.md` and `depth-levels.md`.

## D1 User interviews

| Concept | Practice | Chapter |
|---|---|---|
| **DI01 Problem interviews (the Mom Test).** Ask about their life, not your idea; ask about specific past events, not hypothetical futures; listen more than you talk. The most useful question: "Walk me through the last time you experienced this problem." A leading or hypothetical question produces polite agreement that predicts nothing. | Interview notes in the repo written from the student's own conversations with people who have the problem, with questions about specific past events. | Ch 4: Problem Interviews (The Mom Test); Conducting Effective Interviews |
| **DI02 The evidence ladder: said, did, paid.** What people said is weak evidence, what they did is stronger, what they paid is strongest, because each rung costs more to fake. The past is on the did rung and the future on the said rung, so tag every claim by its rung. | A committed synthesis, insights file or test note in which claims are labeled said, did or paid. | Ch 4: The Evidence Ladder: Said, Did, Paid |
| **DI03 Interview synthesis and saturation.** After several interviews, write the validated problem statement, who you talked to, key insights tagged by rung, quotes, and next steps. Stop interviewing when new interviews stop surprising you, and widen the sample when one person is all you have. | A committed synthesis or insights file that cites the student's own interview notes by file and states what they showed. | Ch 4: Interview Synthesis |

## D2 Problem and who it is for

| Concept | Practice | Chapter |
|---|---|---|
| **DI04 Problem statement and the stranger test.** A problem statement names a person, a situation, and what goes wrong for them today, with no product, feature or technology in it. A stranger who lives it would recognize it; a solution statement only makes sense to someone who heard the pitch (the Idea Trap). | A problem statement in the README, a spec or a synthesis file, written by the student and committed, that names a person, a situation and what goes wrong for them, and names no product or feature. | Ch 3: Why Most Products Fail; Problem vs. Solution Thinking. Ch 4: Problem Journaling |
| **DI05 Jobs to be done.** People hire a product to make progress in a situation: "When I'm [situation], I want to [motivation], so I can [outcome]," with no product in the middle slot. Jobs are functional, emotional and social; pains are physical, functional, financial, emotional and social; a journey map shows where in the job the pain sits. | A job statement (When I am..., I want to..., so I can...) or a journey map of the whole job, written by the student and committed. | Ch 4: Jobs-to-be-Done Framework; The Five Kinds of Pain |
| **DI06 Who it is for, and who it is not for.** Win one narrow segment completely before expanding (Moore): people who share the same habit, talk to each other, and can all be reached. Say who it is not for. Segments that share a job can still differ in situation and pain. | A written target customer, narrow enough to name, with a list of who the product is not for, in the README, a spec or a value proposition, committed. | Ch 4: Who It Is For, and Not For (Start narrow: the beachhead) |
| **DI07 User versus payer.** The person who uses the product and the person who pays for it may differ. The user cares whether it is easy, the payer whether it is worth the money, so you need evidence from both. | A committed file that names the product's user and its payer and says whether they are the same person. | Ch 4: Who It Is For, and Not For (The user and the payer) |
| **DI08 The access test.** A real problem is not an opportunity if you cannot reach the people who have it. Check location (where they gather), engagement (will they respond) and reciprocity (what you offer in return). | A committed note that names where the target users can be reached (places, channels or named groups) and whether the student can reach them. | Ch 4: Can You Reach Them? The Access Test |
| **DI09 Personas.** A fictional but realistic customer that summarizes interview evidence you already have. A persona generated before talking to anyone, by a person or an agent, contains no information and confirms what you already believed. | A personas file written by the student and committed, with each persona's details drawn from named interview notes. | Ch 4: Building Personas |

## D3 Assumption tests

| Concept | Practice | Chapter |
|---|---|---|
| **DI10 Belief versus truth and triangulation.** A map of what you believe, what is true, and how much evidence you have; False Confidence (sure, and wrong) is the most dangerous region. Every way of knowing can mislead, so treat a belief as validated only when independent sources agree. Good evidence would look different if you were wrong. | A committed insights or assumptions file that lists beliefs, each with a confidence level and the evidence for it. | Ch 4: Discovery Is Truth-Seeking (Six ways of knowing, all flawed; Thinking in probabilities) |
| **DI11 Issue trees and MECE.** Break a messy question into parts that do not overlap and leave nothing out (mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive), splitting until each leaf can be checked with evidence. Work first on the branch with high impact, high uncertainty and testability, and skip any question that fails the so-what test (would the answer change what we build?). | A committed issue tree that breaks an open product question into parts, with the part chosen to test marked. | Ch 4: Structure a Messy Problem: Issue Trees |
| **DI12 What would have to be true.** State the idea as a hypothesis on day one, then list the specific conditions that must hold for it to work (Roger Martin). Each condition can be tested; a vague belief cannot. | A committed list of conditions that would have to be true for the product to work, each labeled with its risk or uncertainty. | Ch 4: The Four Risks and the Riskiest Assumption (What would have to be true) |
| **DI13 The four risks.** Value (will they want it), usability (can they use it), feasibility (can we build it), viability (does it work as a business). A MECE list of how a product fails. Teams over-test feasibility and under-test value; a working app proves only that it could be built. | A committed assumptions list with each assumption labeled as a value, usability, feasibility or viability risk. | Ch 4: The Four Risks and the Riskiest Assumption (The four risks) |
| **DI14 Riskiest assumption.** The condition that is both most uncertain and most fatal if false. Test it before anything else, even before a cheaper quick win, because work on other parts is wasted if it fails. | A committed file, outside the sprint plan, that names the product's riskiest assumption, the check run on it, and the result. | Ch 4: The Four Risks and the Riskiest Assumption (The riskiest assumption) |
| **DI15 The smallest test: smoke test, fake door, concierge, Wizard of Oz, presell.** Before building, fake it, do it by hand, or sell it first. Choose by learning per hour: the highest evidence rung on the riskiest assumption for the fewest hours. A working app shown to friends stays on the said rung for value. | A committed note of a smoke test, fake door, concierge test, Wizard of Oz test or presell the student ran, with the action counted and the result. | Ch 4: The Smallest Test |

## D4 Competitive landscape

| Concept | Practice | Chapter |
|---|---|---|
| **DI16 The current habit and the forces of progress.** The main competitor is what the person does today: a spreadsheet, a group text, or nothing. A person switches only when push plus pull outweighs anxiety plus habit (Moesta), so the product must beat the habit by more than the cost of switching. No rival app does not mean no competition. | A committed record, drawn from the student's own interviews, of what people do about the problem today, including doing nothing or a manual workaround. | Ch 4: The Real Competitor Is the Current Habit |
| **DI17 Importance versus satisfaction.** Plot needs by how much they matter and how well current solutions meet them (Olsen). The opportunity is high importance with low satisfaction; high importance with high satisfaction means competing with what already works. | A committed table or chart that rates needs by importance and by satisfaction with current solutions, from interview data. | Ch 4: Evaluating Your Problems (Another lens: importance and satisfaction) |
| **DI18 Five forces.** Rivalry, new entrants and substitutes decide whether an industry can hold price above cost; buyer and supplier power decide whether a neighbor can bargain your share away (Porter). The forces describe the industry, not whether your product is strongest in it. | knowledge only (exams) | Ch 3: The five forces: how wide is the band? |
| **DI19 Blue ocean and value innovation.** Create uncontested space by raising willingness to pay and cutting cost at once, dropping what the industry over-serves and adding what it never offered (Kim and Mauborgne). A blue ocean has no barriers, so it needs a moat to stay yours. | A committed list of the factors the product drops, reduces, raises and adds compared with the alternatives. | Ch 3: Blue oceans: widening the band |
| **DI20 Moats (7 Powers).** Sources of durable advantage (Helmer): network effects, switching costs, scale economies, brand, counter-positioning, cornered resource, process power. Ask what stops the next builder with the same AI tools from copying you next week. An incumbent's switching costs can close an apparent opening. | knowledge only (exams) | Ch 3: Moats: why the win lasts |
| **DI21 Disruption.** Incumbents improve for their most demanding customers and overshoot the rest (sustaining innovation). An entrant starts in a low-end foothold (overserved customers) or a new-market foothold (nonconsumers) and moves upmarket (Christensen). Fast growth that hurts incumbents is not disruption by itself. | knowledge only (exams) | Ch 3: Disruption: how small entrants beat leaders |

## D5 Prioritization

| Concept | Practice | Chapter |
|---|---|---|
| **DI22 Scoring problems and the commitment decision.** Score candidate problems on frequency, intensity, willingness to pay and market size, then check conviction and technical feasibility. Estimate willingness to pay from what the problem costs today, not from a price people name. Commit when five or more interviews show signals above the said rung, the habit to beat is named, and a small test has not disproved the riskiest assumption; watch for solution love, confirmation bias, sunk cost and founder fantasy. | A committed scoring of two or more candidate problems on frequency, intensity, willingness to pay or market size, with the evidence for each score and the problem chosen. | Ch 4: Evaluating Your Problems; The Commitment Decision; Avoiding Premature Commitment. Appendix: Problem Discovery Framework (Scoring) |
| **DI23 MVP and scoping.** The smallest thing that tests the riskiest assumption with real users and produces learning you can act on (Ries); it can be a smoke test or one core feature. Cut to the one thing users must be able to do, then make that excellent (minimum awesome). With fixed time, hold the time and cut scope, stating in writing what is out. | A committed MVP scope that names the one thing users must do and lists what is left out. | Ch 4: What is an MVP?; Minimum Viable, or Minimum Awesome?; MVP Scoping: Deciding What to Cut |
| **DI24 Prioritization frameworks: ICE, MoSCoW, the $100 test.** ICE scores impact times confidence over effort; MoSCoW sorts must, should, could and won't; the $100 test forces an uneven split of a fixed budget. A score is only as good as the evidence behind it: a high confidence rating with no evidence is a guess. | A committed ranking of features or work items in order, with the criteria or framework (ICE, MoSCoW, the $100 test) shown. | Ch 4: Feature Prioritization |
| **DI25 Continuous discovery and the opportunity solution tree.** Keep talking to customers every sprint (Torres). The tree runs outcome, opportunities, solutions, experiments; every solution must sit under an opportunity you actually discovered, and every experiment tests one assumption behind a solution. | A committed opportunity solution tree for the product (outcome, opportunities, solutions, tests), with each opportunity traced to interview notes. | Ch 4: Discovery Is Continuous |

## D6 What to build

| Concept | Practice | Chapter |
|---|---|---|
| **DI26 Product strategy: vision, where to play, how to win, what not to do.** A vision describes the better world the product creates (Cagan). Strategy is a small set of hard choices, including what not to do (Porter); for a student product the key choices are where to play and how to win (Lafley and Martin). Building is cheap now, so a small market can be worth it. | A committed one-page product strategy that states the vision, where the product plays, how it wins, and what it will not do. | Ch 3: Vision before strategy; Strategy is choosing what not to do; Where to play, and how to win; Putting it to work |
| **DI27 Diverge before you converge.** Generate many candidate problems before judging any, then narrow on purpose. A problem journal captures every friction without filtering; seizing the first idea is premature convergence. | A committed list of several candidate problems or ideas and a written choice of one over at least one other, with the reason. | Ch 4: You Are Your Best First Customer; Problem Journaling; Diverge Before You Converge |
| **DI28 The value stick: creating and capturing value.** Value created is willingness to pay minus willingness to sell (Brandenburger and Stuart). Price and cost divide it into what the customer keeps, what the maker captures, and what suppliers keep. Creating value does not guarantee capturing it; industry structure limits the share. | A committed value stick for the product with willingness to pay, price, cost and willingness to sell each estimated. | Ch 3: Creating value, and capturing it |
| **DI29 DHM and the value proposition.** DHM (Biddle): a good strategy delights customers in hard-to-copy, margin-enhancing ways, all three at once. The value proposition template states for whom, which problem, the category, the benefit, unlike which alternatives (the current habit first), and the differentiator; the Value Proposition Canvas and Bain's Elements of Value check it against the customer's jobs, pains and gains. | A value proposition the student wrote beyond the template's text, naming the customer and the current habit the product replaces, committed. | Ch 3: A test worth memorizing: DHM; Creating value, and capturing it (Elements of Value). Ch 4: Your Value Proposition |
| **DI30 The reflection test and the directional test.** Habit-forming products run trigger, action, variable reward, investment (Eyal); the mechanics are neutral. The test: would the user, in a calm and reflective moment, be glad you designed it this way? For the needs it touches, does the product expand agency, support becoming, and deepen connection? | A committed check of the product's habit loop or engagement design against the reflection test and the directional test. | Ch 3: Understanding human nature: to serve users or to exploit them; Aiming well: virtue and the golden mean. Appendix: Problem Discovery Framework (Gospel Framework) |

## Book gaps

- Decision records: D1, D3, D4 and D6 level 5 all require a decisions/ file that cites interviews or a test, but no chapter shows how to write one (options, deciding reason, evidence cited, committed before the work). Add a short section to Ch 4 after The Commitment Decision, or in Ch 6b with specs.
- Writing up a comparison of alternatives (D4 level 4): Ch 4 teaches the current habit and Ch 3 teaches five forces and moats, but nothing shows how to build a comparison table of what users do today from interview notes, as distinct from a desk-research competitor list. Add a worked table to Ch 4: The Real Competitor Is the Current Habit.
- Choosing one idea over another (D6 level 4): Diverge Before You Converge and Evaluating Your Problems score problems, but no section shows a written idea-versus-idea choice that names who it is for and why it can win. Add to Ch 4: The Commitment Decision, with an example record.
- Stopping an idea: Signs You Should Pivot lists symptoms, but there is no guidance on recording a decision to stop or shelve an idea from evidence (the pilot found such records fit no item). Add to Ch 4: Avoiding Premature Commitment.
- Ranking with criteria shown (D5 level 4): Feature Prioritization lists ICE, MoSCoW and the $100 test without a worked ranked list, and carries a MOVE note saying it belongs with specs. Add a worked example and settle where the section lives.
- Opportunity solution tree is taught in one paragraph and a figure in Discovery Is Continuous, but has a midterm question. Expand it, or retire the question.
- Surveys: D3 level 4 lists a survey as a valid check, but the book does not teach survey design or when a survey misleads (only that a concierge test teaches more than a survey). Add a short note to Ch 4: The Smallest Test.
- The brief (Ch 4: From Discovery to the Brief) is left to G4 Specs on the Design axis; if Design does not claim it, it needs a Discovery concept.

## Evidence a packet or the session scan cannot see yet

- Interview order: an interview counts as problem-interview evidence only if it happened before the student showed the product, and an interview that only collects the product's content does not count. Both need the interview text and date. The packet often lists discovery/ files with no text (the text budget runs out). Put discovery/ files ahead of code in the packet's text budget.
- Assumption named before the check (assumption-test practice) needs commit order between the assumptions file and the test note. The packet shows commit hashes but a coder cannot always tell which came first; listing each discovery file's first-commit date in the packet would settle it.
- Practice for most Discovery concepts happens outside the repo and outside Claude Code (talking to people, a whiteboard). The session scan sees only an agent asked to draft or check a guide, a persona, a tree or a ranking. A "Discovery this sprint" table in the /sprint-review template (what was done, with whom, date, file) would give the scan something to read.
- Agent-written versus student-written: a persona, synthesis or assumptions list drafted by an agent from nothing looks the same in the repo as one built from interviews. The session scan could flag discovery files an agent wrote before any interview note existed.
- For depth level 4 (Delivered) on Discovery, the people involved are often interviewees, who have not used the product. The packet cannot tell an interviewee from a user; the review's "Users this sprint" list separates them.
- Smoke tests and presells record their result as counts (visits, sign-ups, deposits) that live in a landing-page tool or Stripe, not the repo. Ask students to commit the counts with a date in the test note.
- Several Ch 3 concepts (five forces, blue ocean, moats, disruption, value stick, DHM, reflection test) are rarely written down in a student repo; their practice evidence rests on the one-page product strategy. Asking for that page as a committed strategy.md in Sprint 2 would make them visible.
